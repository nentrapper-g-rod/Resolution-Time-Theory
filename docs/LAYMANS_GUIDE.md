# Resolution Time Theory — Explained Simply (Layman's Version)

**Author:** Joshua B. Girod

This is a plain-language explanation of Resolution Time Theory (RTT).  
No advanced math required. For the technical version, see the Core Edition paper.

---

## The big idea

Quantum mechanics says tiny particles behave in weird, random-looking ways. RTT asks:

**What if those weird statistics are not the deepest truth — what if they are what we see when our detectors are too slow to follow something that is actually moving very fast and regularly?**

Like a camera with a long shutter speed photographing a spinning fan: the blades look like a blurry disk. The blur is real as a *picture*, but the fan is not really a disk.

---

## What the theory builds

### 1. A classical wave makes the stripe pattern

In the famous double-slit experiment, two waves overlap and make bright and dark stripes.  
RTT says: that pattern can come from an ordinary high-frequency wave. You do not need quantum magic for the *pattern itself*.

### 2. Particles try to sit on the bright stripes

Experiments show particles end up where the bright stripes are.  

A normal force from the wave does **not** automatically put them in the right places forever.  
So RTT adds **one extra rule** (a postulate): a special kind of effective potential related to the brightness.  

Once that rule is in place, the particles lock onto the stripes exactly. Math proves it; computer simulations confirm it.

### 3. Free clouds of particles spread the right way

When there are no stripes, a little cloud of particles should grow the way quantum mechanics says.  
RTT borrows a known classical random-motion model (Nelson's) that already does that.  
This part is borrowed, not invented by RTT — and the paper says so clearly.

### 4. Slow detectors blur the answer

If your detector only looks for a short or long window of time, the stripe contrast can fade.  

RTT connects the important time scale to something real in the lab: how long it takes an electron to travel the extra path length between the two routes. That time is set by the machine's geometry and the electron's speed — not picked just to make the theory look good.

### 5. A possible test

Run a pulsed electron experiment and change how long the detector's "gate" is open.  

Ordinary quantum mechanics and RTT can predict *slightly different* curves for how the stripes fade.  
If the curves really differ in a real experiment, that would be interesting. If they do not, RTT is in trouble in that area.

### 6. Distant particles (Bell experiments)

To match the famous Bell experiments, RTT allows a shared universal clock so the two sides can stay coordinated.  
That is a strong assumption (similar to some other hidden-variable theories). The paper states it openly instead of hiding it.

---

## What RTT is *not* saying

- It is **not** saying quantum mechanics is wrong about experimental numbers.  
- It is **not** saying it has fully derived quantum mechanics from a classical wave.  
- It is **not** saying it has explained relativity or time dilation yet.

It **is** saying: here is a possible classical picture, here is the exact place it needs an extra rule, here is a lab-style comparison that might test part of it, and here is what is still unsolved.

---

## The heart of the theory in one sentence

**A fast classical wave can make the stripe pattern, but getting the particles to sit on those stripes forever requires one extra rule that the wave alone does not give you — and that gap is the real problem the theory still has to solve.**

That honest statement is what makes the work stronger than many speculative ideas: it shows exactly where the hard part is instead of pretending the hard part is already finished.
